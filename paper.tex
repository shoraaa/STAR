\begin{abstract}
Neural routing policies are typically trained on short autoregressive trajectories, but at test time they are often deployed as full-trajectory generators or broad reconstruction modules. This creates an out-of-distribution inference process: as the decoded horizon grows, intermediate states drift away from the local training distribution, and the objective feedback becomes global and delayed. We introduce \method{} (\methodfull{}), a training-free inference guidance framework that aligns test-time queries with the scale at which pretrained policies are reliable. Rather than retraining the neural policy, \method{} repeatedly queries a pretrained model on route-conditioned local states, projects each proposal back to a feasible trajectory through \BTPfull{} (\BTP{}), and stores positive local advantages in \EAMfull{} (\EAM{}) as a non-parametric contextual prior for future logit shaping. Thus, extra inference compute becomes stateful, instance-specific guidance without gradient updates or weight changes. Using \SIL{} \citep{sil} as the base autoregressive policy, our method solves all evaluated \TSP{} and \CVRP{} instances below 10K nodes, achieving 0.36\% and 3.42\% average \TSP{} gaps on the below-1K and 1K--10K groups, and improving substantially over autoregressive construction and reconstruction baselines on \CVRP{}. These results show that aligning the test-time query distribution, rather than retraining the base model, can substantially improve zero-shot neural routing.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Vehicle routing problems are central combinatorial optimization tasks with applications in transportation, logistics, and supply-chain management. The traveling salesman problem (\TSP{}) seeks a minimum-length tour visiting each customer once, while the capacitated vehicle routing problem (\CVRP{}) further introduces depot and capacity constraints. Exact algorithms and handcrafted heuristics can produce high-quality solutions, but strong routing solvers typically require substantial domain expertise and carefully engineered search components.

Neural combinatorial optimization (\NCO{}) offers a learning-based alternative. Instead of manually designing every search component, an \NCO{} method trains a model on routing instances and uses the trained model to produce solutions for unseen instances. Once trained, such models can generate solutions quickly, which makes them attractive for large collections of routing instances. Existing neural routing methods can be broadly categorized into constructive policies, reconstruction-based inference, decomposition or local-policy methods, projection-based generalization methods, and learned improvement pipelines. Autoregressive (\AR{}) construction is especially common: given an instance, a partial route, and a feasibility mask, the model predicts a distribution over the next node. Early constructive work established sequence-to-sequence and attention-based decoding for routing (e.g., Pointer Networks and attention models \citep{pointernetwork,attentionmodel}); multi-start training and inference improve solution diversity (e.g., POMO \citep{pomo}); decoder-heavy architectures improve large-scale generalization (e.g., BQ and \LEHD{} \citep{bq,lehd}); and self-improved training with reconstruction strengthens pretrained policies (e.g., \SIL{} \citep{sil}).

However, models trained on small routing instances often degrade when applied to larger or more structured instances. Scaling the training distribution is one option, but it requires stronger labels, more expensive rollouts, and larger search spaces. A complementary option is to keep a strong small-scale model fixed and allocate more computation at test time. For this strategy to work, the inference procedure must keep the model's test-time query distribution close to the local states on which its decisions remain reliable, rather than merely drawing independent samples from the same policy.

Existing test-time scaling methods expose two complementary OOD generalization issues. Projection-based work shows that, after restricting the search space to a local candidate subgraph, the distribution presented to the model can differ sharply from the small uniform instances used for training \citep{ttpl}. This motivates alignment of the model input distribution. We study a different axis of the same phenomenon: even when the action interface is preserved, standard inference induces covariate shift through the sequence of states on which the policy is queried. One-shot construction forces a localized next-node policy to autoregressively decode a long global trajectory, so early errors alter later states. Broad reconstruction reduces the horizon but still evaluates a large coupled subproblem with sparse credit assignment. The core issue is therefore an inference-unit mismatch that drives length-extrapolation and state-distribution shift.

To address this mismatch, we propose \method{} (\methodfull{}), a training-free inference guidance framework for pretrained neural routing policies. The framework maintains a reference trajectory, the best solution found so far, and a non-parametric episodic memory over structural transitions. At each iteration, it initializes a route-conditioned state, queries the neural policy for a bounded sequence of autoregressive transitions, applies \BTPfull{} (\BTP{}) to restore feasibility and local consistency, and updates \EAMfull{} (\EAM{}) from transitions with positive local advantage. \EAM{} then acts as a contextual prior that shapes future logits. The mechanism performs no backpropagation and changes no neural weights, while assigning feedback to the local structure modified by the bounded transition.

Comprehensive experiments are conducted on \TSP{} and \CVRP{} instances below 10K nodes by applying the framework to a pretrained \SIL{} policy. The results show that our method improves over construction-only neural inference and is especially effective on \CVRP{}, where the value of a transition depends on capacity, depot structure, and neighboring route interactions. Diagnostic experiments study inference scaling, component importance, memory design, scope sensitivity, and plug-and-play behavior across pretrained neural policies. These analyses show that local transition inference is not only a high-performing routing procedure, but also a training-free mechanism for improving OOD generalization of pretrained autoregressive models.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
  \item We formulate inference-unit mismatch as a source of OOD length-extrapolation failure in pretrained autoregressive routing: construction and broad reconstruction induce query distributions whose horizon and feedback structure differ from the local states used during training.
  \item We introduce a training-free inference guidance framework that combines route-conditioned local decoding, \BTP{}, and \EAM{}.
  \item We provide mechanistic and empirical evidence for the proposed inference unit, including inference-scaling, OOD length-extrapolation, credit-density, component-wise, memory-design, and scope-sensitivity analyses.
  \item We evaluate the framework on top of a pretrained \SIL{} policy \citep{sil}, showing that bounded transition guidance is much faster than high-budget autoregressive reconstruction while remaining competitive on \TSP{} and strong on \CVRP{}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

Neural routing methods can be broadly grouped into five paradigms: direct autoregressive construction, reconstruction-based inference, decomposition or local-policy generalization, projection-based test-time generalization, and learned improvement. The proposed framework operates on constructive autoregressive policies and modifies only the inference procedure.

\subsection{Autoregressive Neural Construction}

Autoregressive models construct routing solutions one decision at a time. This paradigm includes sequence-to-sequence construction and attention-based decoding (e.g., Pointer Networks and attention models \citep{pointernetwork,attentionmodel}). Subsequent work improves the constructive policy through complementary mechanisms: training and inference over multiple equivalent optima for solution diversity (e.g., POMO \citep{pomo}); decoder-heavy architectures and supervised-learning design for large-scale generalization (e.g., BQ and \LEHD{} \citep{bq,lehd}); and self-improved learning with reconstruction-oriented inference (e.g., \SIL{} \citep{sil}). These methods primarily improve the policy, training procedure, or reconstruction routine; our framework keeps the pretrained policy fixed and changes the distribution of test-time policy queries.

\subsection{Reconstruction at Inference Time}

Reconstruction-based inference conditions the policy on an existing solution instead of constructing a route only once. Random partial-solution reconstruction samples a segment from the current route, reconstructs it with the decoder, and accepts the proposal when it improves the complete solution (e.g., \RRC{} in \LEHD{} \citep{lehd}). Parallel local reconstruction samples multiple non-overlapping partial solutions and reconstructs them in parallel for efficient multi-round inference (e.g., \PRC{} in \SIL{} \citep{sil}). These methods show that autoregressive decoders contain useful conditional structure beyond greedy construction. Our framework uses the same conditional policy interface but reduces the query unit from a broad reconstructed segment to a bounded local transition. The resulting proposal is projected back to a feasible locally corrected trajectory, and its positive local advantage is converted into future logit bias.

\subsection{Decomposition, Local Policies, and Projection}

Decomposition and local-policy methods reduce the effective problem size before applying a neural decision rule. Some methods decompose or localize the instance before applying a neural solver (e.g., H-TSP and GLOP \citep{htsp,glop}). Others restrict each decision to a candidate neighborhood and use invariant views to improve generalization (e.g., INViT \citep{invit}). Projection-based methods address a related distributional issue: the selected local subgraphs can differ substantially from the small uniform instances used during training. Coordinate projection can align candidate subgraphs with the training distribution and combine projected views with decision fusion and reconstruction at inference time (e.g., TTPL \citep{ttpl}).

While projection-based inference addresses test-time mismatch by aligning the input feature distribution through coordinate projections (e.g., TTPL \citep{ttpl}), the proposed framework addresses mismatch in the policy query distribution. Specifically, the framework preserves the autoregressive action interface, evaluates bounded transitions inside a reference trajectory, and uses positive local feedback to bias future calls on the same instance. Projection methods align the input presented to the model; our method aligns the scale and state distribution of the autoregressive query.

\subsection{Neural Guidance for Improvement}

Learned-improvement methods embed neural predictions inside stronger optimization pipelines, using learned signals to guide search or improvement procedures (e.g., DIFUSCO, GFACS, and NeuroLKH \citep{difusco,gfacs,neurolkh}). Our approach also uses learned information during test-time optimization, but preserves the autoregressive action interface. The episodic memory maps local feedback to logit shaping for future model calls.

\subsection{Training-Free Inference Guidance}

Training-free inference guidance studies how an already trained model can be used more effectively on a test instance without updating its parameters. In routing, a single instance can be queried many times, and the value of a local decision is only observed after it interacts with the trajectory. This creates an inference-scaling problem: additional test-time compute improves solutions only when the inference loop converts repeated model calls into stateful guidance rather than independent rollouts. Our framework implements this guidance through direct logit shaping from non-parametric episodic memory.

\section{Preliminaries}

\subsection{Vehicle Routing Problems}

Vehicle routing problems require a feasible traversal over a set of customers under problem-specific constraints. In the traveling salesman problem (\TSP{}), the solution is a single tour visiting every customer exactly once. In the capacitated vehicle routing problem (\CVRP{}), the solution consists of multiple depot-rooted routes whose customer demands must satisfy vehicle-capacity constraints. Let $x$ be a Euclidean routing instance on node set $V=\{0,\ldots,n-1\}$. A feasible solution $y\in\cY(x)$ induces an edge set or multiset $E(y)$, and its objective value is
\begin{equation}
  \cost_x(y)=\sum_{(i,j)\in E(y)}d_x(i,j),
  \label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
where $d_x(i,j)$ denotes the benchmark distance between nodes $i$ and $j$. The objective is to find a feasible $y$ minimizing $\cost_x(y)$. Given a reference cost $\cost_x^\star$, the percentage optimality gap is
\begin{equation}
  \gap_x(y)=100\cdot\frac{\cost_x(y)-\cost_x^\star}{\cost_x^\star}.
  \label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Autoregressive Solution Construction}

Constructive \NCO{} replaces hand-designed construction rules with a learned policy. An autoregressive model with parameters $\theta$ constructs a solution through states $s_t$ and actions $a_t$:
\begin{equation}
  P_\theta(y\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{T(x,y)}P_\theta(a_t\mid x,s_t),
  \qquad a_t\in\cA(x,s_t).
  \label{eq:ar}
\end{equation}
The state contains the partial route, current node, visited mask, and feasibility variables; $\cA(x,s_t)$ is the feasible action set. The pretrained policy logit for action $a$ is denoted by $\ell_\theta(a\mid x,s)$. Standard greedy inference selects
\begin{equation}
  a_t=\argmax_{a\in\cA(x,s_t)}\ell_\theta(a\mid x,s_t),
  \label{eq:greedy}
\end{equation}
until a complete trajectory is formed. Sampling-based inference instead draws $a_t\sim P_\theta(\cdot\mid x,s_t)$ and returns the best complete trajectory among multiple independent rollouts. Both modes use the pretrained policy as a full-trajectory generator: the number of neural decisions grows with $T(x,y)$, which increases substantially under length extrapolation.

\subsection{Inference-Time Search and Reconstruction}

Inference-time search allocates additional compute after training while keeping $\theta$ fixed. The most direct form is multi-start sampling, which repeats Eq.~\eqref{eq:ar} from different random choices and selects the lowest-cost feasible solution. A more structured approach is reconstruction: given a reference trajectory $y$, a partial segment is removed, the autoregressive policy reconstructs the missing part under boundary and feasibility constraints, and the proposal replaces the original segment when it improves the objective.

Recent LNS-inspired neural methods instantiate this reconstruction paradigm at different granularities. Random partial-solution reconstruction samples a segment from the current route, reconstructs it with the neural decoder, and accepts the reconstructed segment if it improves the complete solution (e.g., \RRC{} in \LEHD{} \citep{lehd}). Parallel local reconstruction samples multiple non-overlapping partial solutions and reconstructs them in parallel, improving the efficiency of multi-round inference while preserving feasibility for \TSP{} and \CVRP{} (e.g., \PRC{} in \SIL{} \citep{sil}). These methods demonstrate that pretrained autoregressive policies contain useful conditional structure beyond one-shot construction.

The present work also operates in this frozen-policy inference setting: the neural parameters are not updated, while external test-time states such as reference trajectories, candidate lists, and non-parametric contextual memory may change. The next section analyzes the OOD query-distribution effects induced by full-trajectory generation and broad reconstruction, motivating a bounded local transition interface.

\section{OOD Query Distributions}
\label{sec:bottleneck}

Performance degradation under length extrapolation does not imply that the pretrained policy fails at local decision-making. The policy remains useful because it scores feasible local actions conditioned on a partial route state. Standard inference instead applies this local decision rule outside its most reliable query distribution. Full construction and broad reconstruction induce test-time state distributions with longer horizons, larger coupled subproblems, and coarser feedback than the policy saw during training.

Table~\ref{tab:ood-motivation} reports the resulting length-extrapolation pattern. Greedy decoding and independent sampling improve small instances but degrade as the trajectory length grows, consistent with covariate shift during long autoregressive decoding. Broad reconstruction reduces this degradation, but still evaluates a large coupled subproblem. Bounded local transitions keep the neural horizon fixed and improve length extrapolation by repeatedly evaluating route-conditioned local query states.

\paragraph{Length-extrapolation covariate shift.}
In a full construction trajectory, the number of autoregressive decisions grows with the instance and route length. If $T(x,y)$ is the number of actions required to decode solution $y$, then one-shot construction queries the policy for $T(x,y)$ sequential decisions as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ar}. Under scale shift, each additional decision can alter the future state distribution, so later queries can move farther from the training distribution. In contrast, local transition inference performs at most $r$ decisions in a route-conditioned state, with $r\ll T(x,y)$ on large instances. The model is still evaluated through its learned action interface, but the requested horizon is bounded by the inference unit rather than by the full problem scale.

\paragraph{Credit dilution.}
Broad reconstruction can modify many route relations before receiving a single scalar outcome. Let $q(\hat y,y)=|E(\hat y)\triangle E(y)|$ be the number of changed relations between a reference trajectory $y$ and a proposal $\hat y$, and let $\Delta C=\cost_x(y)-\cost_x(\hat y)$ be the observed improvement. The average proposal-level credit available per changed relation is then proportional to
\begin{equation}
  \rho(\hat y;y)=\frac{\Delta C}{\max(1,q(\hat y,y))}.
  \label{eq:credit-density}
\end{equation}
As $q$ grows, the same scalar reward becomes a weaker signal about any particular neural decision. A useful training-free guidance unit should keep $q$ small enough that positive feedback can be assigned to the structural changes that caused the improvement.

\paragraph{Local distribution and constraints.}
A model trained on small uniformly generated instances is not only extrapolated to longer routes; during scalable inference it is often evaluated on neighborhoods induced by the current partial route, reference trajectory, or candidate list. Projection-based test-time generalization makes this point explicit for KNN-based construction: the candidate subgraph can differ substantially from the training distribution, so projecting the subgraph back toward a uniform coordinate distribution improves the pretrained model's decisions (e.g., TTPL \citep{ttpl}). Our framework follows the same OOD diagnosis but modifies the query interface rather than the coordinate system. It constructs a route-conditioned local state, applies \BTP{} to the decoded transition, and feeds positive structural changes back through episodic memory. This is especially important for \CVRP{}, where a useful transition must also satisfy capacity, depot, and route-boundary interactions.

The resulting inference unit has three properties. It is context preserving because the pretrained policy receives a construction-compatible state and a feasible-action mask. It is scope bounded because the transition budget controls how many new route relations are introduced. It is feedback carrying because successful local transitions influence future model calls through a dynamic test-time prior.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Training-Free Guidance Framework}

We maintain a reference trajectory $y_t$, a best-so-far trajectory $y_t^\star$, and a non-parametric episodic memory $M_t:\cE\rightarrow[-M_{\max},M_{\max}]$. Each iteration instantiates one guidance unit: it selects a route-conditioned state, queries the neural policy for a bounded autoregressive transition, projects the resulting trajectory back to local feasibility and consistency, moves the reference trajectory to the best projected proposal, and updates memory from proposals with positive advantage over the reference.

The framework separates three components that correspond to the OOD query-distribution argument. The neural model provides conditional structure by ranking feasible next nodes in a local state. \BTP{} restores feasibility and short-range consistency after the neural transition. \EAM{} provides a non-parametric contextual prior that biases future local decisions toward transitions that have already shown positive advantage on the same instance. These components have complementary computational profiles: neural decoding proposes structured local transitions, trajectory projection evaluates their immediate geometric and feasibility consequences, and memory accumulates instance-specific evidence without modifying the neural parameters.

The memory is external to the neural network. It can be initialized, cleared, clipped, or inspected without changing the model parameters. Thus, the framework can be applied to pretrained checkpoints and to settings where retraining or gradient-based test-time adaptation is unavailable or undesirable.

\subsection{Route-Conditioned Local Decoding}

At iteration $t$, let $u_t$ be the current route anchor and let $C_t\subseteq V$ be the feasible candidate set exposed to the decoder. The local state is
\begin{equation}
  s^{\CPP}_0=(x,\ y_t,\ u_t,\ C_t,\ \text{mask},\ \text{feasibility state}).
  \label{eq:cpp}
\end{equation}
The decoder selects a short sequence $\sigma_t=(a_1,\ldots,a_m)$, with $m$ controlled by a transition budget $r$. Each selected action updates the local route-conditioned state and introduces a short chain of new route relations,
\begin{equation}
  (u_t,a_1),(a_1,a_2),\ldots,(a_{m-1},a_m),
  \label{eq:splice}
\end{equation}
while the rest of the reference trajectory supplies the surrounding context before projection.

The state in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cpp} is a restricted construction state. The route context, current anchor, visited mask, and feasible-action mask are represented in the same form as ordinary autoregressive decoding, while the reference trajectory supplies global context. The model therefore selects plausible transitions within a small feasible set under its learned sequential structure. By construction, every local decode has a bounded horizon, independent of the global instance size.

For \TSP{}, this corresponds to a local autoregressive transition over customer order. For \CVRP{}, the same principle is applied with route-boundary information and remaining capacity in the local mask, so the transition can be evaluated by capacity-preserving projection.

\subsection{Episodic Memory and Dynamic Logit Shaping}

Let $\phi(s,a)$ be the set of edges introduced when action $a$ is selected in state $s$. The memory score is
\begin{equation}
  G_t(s,a)=\frac{1}{\max(1,|\phi(s,a)|)}\sum_{e\in\phi(s,a)}M_t(e).
  \label{eq:memory-score}
\end{equation}
The dynamic logit-shaping distribution is defined as
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde P_t(a\mid x,s)=
  \frac{
  \exp((\ell_\theta(a\mid x,s)+\lambda G_t(s,a)-\beta\Delta c(s,a))/\tau)
  }{
  \sum_{b\in\cA(x,s)}
  \exp((\ell_\theta(b\mid x,s)+\lambda G_t(s,b)-\beta\Delta c(s,b))/\tau)
  }.
  \label{eq:shaped}
\end{equation}
Here $\lambda\ge0$ controls the strength of episodic memory, $\beta\ge0$ controls the distance feature, and $\tau>0$ denotes the sampling temperature. These values are fixed within a run unless explicitly varied in a sensitivity study. The feature $\Delta c(s,a)$ is a normalized one-step distance proxy. If $p_s$ denotes the current endpoint in the local transition, we compute
\begin{equation}
  \Delta c(s,a)=\frac{d_x(p_s,a)}{\bar d_x},
  \label{eq:insertion}
\end{equation}
where $\bar d_x$ is the mean distance over the local candidate list. This term is not a learned component; it is a test-time geometric prior that discourages obviously expensive transitions when the masked action set contains several plausible neural choices.

Given a reference trajectory $y_t$ and projected candidate $\hat y_t$, define
\begin{equation}
  A_t(\hat y_t;y_t)=
  \frac{\cost_x(y_t)-\cost_x(\hat y_t)}{\max(\cost_x(y_t),\epsilon)}
  \label{eq:advantage}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon>0$ is a small numerical constant that prevents division by zero. Define the introduced edges as
\begin{equation}
  \Delta E_t^+=E(\hat y_t)\setminus E(y_t).
  \label{eq:introduced}
\end{equation}
\EAM{} updates episodic memory according to
\begin{equation}
  M_{t+1}(e)=
  \begin{cases}
  \clip(M_t(e)+\eta A_t(\hat y_t;y_t),-M_{\max},M_{\max}),
  & \text{if } A_t(\hat y_t;y_t)>0 \text{ and } e\in\Delta E_t^+,\\
  M_t(e), & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:update}
\end{equation}
where $\eta>0$ is the memory step size.

The update is positive-only in the reported experiments. Non-improving proposals are not treated as negative evidence because an unsuccessful projected trajectory may arise from the selected state, the decoded sequence, the projection width, or the quality of the reference trajectory. In contrast, a proposal with positive advantage provides a cleaner credit signal. The clipping threshold prevents a small number of early improvements from dominating the pretrained logits throughout inference.

The memory score in Eq.~\eqref{eq:memory-score} is normalized by the number of introduced edges represented by the action. This avoids assigning larger scores to actions only because they touch more memory entries. Since every memory entry is clipped to $[-M_{\max},M_{\max}]$, the absolute memory-induced logit shift is bounded by $\lambda M_{\max}$. Thus, \EAM{} implements bounded, training-free inference guidance: it can shape the pretrained policy distribution without silently replacing its learned logits unless $\lambda$ is deliberately set large.

\subsection{Trajectory Projection via \BTP{}}

Let $D_t$ and $I_t$ denote the deleted and inserted edges from a local transition. The affected endpoints are
\begin{equation}
  A_t^{\mathrm{end}}=\{i:\exists j,\ (i,j)\in D_t\cup I_t \text{ or } (j,i)\in D_t\cup I_t\}.
  \label{eq:endpoints}
\end{equation}
With candidate lists $\cN_K(i)$, the projection region is
\begin{equation}
  \Omega_t=A_t^{\mathrm{end}}\cup\bigcup_{i\in A_t^{\mathrm{end}}}\cN_K(i).
  \label{eq:scope}
\end{equation}
The operator set is fixed and deliberately small. For \TSP{}, \BTP{} applies candidate-graph 2-opt segment reversals whose tested edges touch $\Omega_t$. For \CVRP{}, \BTP{} uses the same intra-route 2-opt projection and additionally evaluates capacity-feasible inter-route relocate, swap, and 2-opt* tail-exchange moves. All moves are implemented directly inside the projection routine; \BTP{} is not a call to LKH or another external solver. Appendix~\ref{app:btp-details} gives the complete checklist procedure.

The operator follows the locality principle of the bounded correction mechanism used in \DyNACO{} \citep{dynaco}, where local correction was introduced for perturbation-based ACO guided by non-autoregressive heatmap-based neural signals. In that setting, the neural output is an edge-level prior inside an ACO backend, and local correction preserves credit assignment by modifying only the neighborhood touched by an ant perturbation. Here, the neural proposal is instead produced by an autoregressive decoder over a masked local construction state.

The role of projection differs across these settings. In \DyNACO{}, local correction preserves the relationship between heatmap-guided edge choices and post-correction cost. In our setting, \BTP{} preserves the relationship between a decoded local sequence and the projected route transition. Accordingly, the projection region is tied to the deleted and inserted endpoints from the autoregressive transition. The operator restores local feasibility and geometry for candidates generated by the neural policy rather than replacing the model with a separate proposal mechanism.

\BTP{} serves as a trajectory projection step rather than an unconstrained improvement solver. Its role is to make each neural proposal locally sharp enough for evaluation. A proposal that introduces promising edges in an awkward order can often be corrected around the changed edges. Conversely, a bounded transition should not damage distant parts of the route. For this reason, the projection region is defined by deleted and inserted endpoints rather than by a fixed route-order window. If each transition introduces at most $r$ new route relations and each affected endpoint exposes at most $K$ candidate neighbors, the number of evaluated move candidates per pass is tied to $r$ and $K$, rather than to the global instance size.

\subsection{Reference Update and Algorithmic Properties}

The reported experiments use a best-proposal reference update with monotone best-so-far tracking:
\begin{equation}
  y_{t+1}=
  \argmin_{\hat y\in\widehat{\cY}_t}\cost_x(\hat y),
  \label{eq:accept}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat{\cY}_t$ is the set of projected proposals generated from reference trajectory $y_t$. The best-so-far solution is updated separately,
\begin{equation}
  y_{t+1}^\star=
  \begin{cases}
  y_{t+1}, & \cost_x(y_{t+1})<\cost_x(y_t^\star),\\
  y_t^\star, & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:best}
\end{equation}
Thus the reference trajectory can move to a different projected proposal for exploration, while the returned solution remains monotone.

Several useful properties follow directly from the algorithm design. First, the local decoding horizon is bounded by the transition budget $r$. Second, episodic memory values remain bounded because every update is clipped to $[-M_{\max},M_{\max}]$. Third, for fixed outer budget $B$, proposal count $S$, and transition budget $r$, the method performs at most $BSr$ autoregressive action selections, apart from encoder reuse. Fourth, best-so-far tracking gives an anytime output even when the reference trajectory is allowed to move for exploration. These properties characterize the computational and guidance behavior of the inference loop.

\subsection{Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Inference algorithm with a pretrained autoregressive policy.}
\label{alg:star}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Instance $x$, pretrained policy $P_\theta$, initial solution $y_0$, budget $B$, transition budget $r$, candidate width $K$, proposals $S$
\STATE Initialize reference trajectory $y \leftarrow y_0$, best trajectory $y^\star \leftarrow y_0$, and episodic memory $M(e)\leftarrow 0$
\STATE Build candidate lists $\cN_K(i)$
\FOR{$t=1,\ldots,B$}
    \STATE Select route-conditioned states and candidate masks for $S$ local transitions
    \STATE Decode bounded transition sequences under the logit-shaped distribution in Eq.~\eqref{eq:shaped}
    \STATE Apply each transition to the reference trajectory
    \STATE Project each candidate with \BTP{} within $\Omega_t$
    \STATE Let $\hat y_t$ be the best projected candidate
    \STATE Update $M$ with \EAM{} from proposals with positive advantage over $y$
    \STATE $y\leftarrow \hat y_t$
    \IF{$\cost_x(y)<\cost_x(y^\star)$}
        \STATE $y^\star\leftarrow y$
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\STATE \textbf{return} $y^\star$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

Our evaluation aims to demonstrate that the proposed inference interface: (1) improves the zero-shot solution quality of pretrained autoregressive policies on large \TSP{} and \CVRP{} instances; (2) converts additional test-time compute into solution improvement more efficiently than independent sampling and broad reconstruction; (3) aligns the policy query distribution with the local decision structure learned during training; (4) derives complementary gains from neural local decoding, \BTP{}, and \EAM{}; and (5) remains stable across transition budgets, projection widths, and memory strengths.

\subsection{Problem Setting}

We adapt the generalization-focused evaluation pipeline proposed in the neural routing solver survey \citep{nrs_survey}. That survey argues that the conventional protocol, which often evaluates on synthetic uniformly distributed instances at a small number of scales, is not sufficient for judging neural routing methods: it can conflate scalability with generalization, mix single-model and multi-model settings, and favor models that overfit to narrow training distributions. The proposed pipeline instead evaluates zero-shot in-problem generalization on benchmark and challenge instances with diverse distributions, standardized inference settings, and complementary effectiveness, efficiency, and reliability metrics.

The empirical evaluation uses this benchmark grouping for \TSP{} and \CVRP{}. The small group contains instances with fewer than 1K nodes, the medium group contains instances from 1K to 10K nodes, and the baseline comparison also reports results from 10K to 100K nodes for methods whose large-scale runs are available in the benchmark. We additionally run the closest projection-based test-time generalization baseline, TTPL \citep{ttpl}, on the TSPLIB \citep{tsplib} and CVRPLIB \citep{cvrplib} instances shared with our evaluation, because those datasets test real-world structure rather than only synthetic random coordinates. This protocol evaluates training-free inference guidance under increasing instance size and routing constraints, rather than only under an in-distribution random-instance protocol.

\subsection{Model and Inference Setting}

For the main experiments, we adopt Self-Improved Learning \citep{sil} as the base autoregressive policy. This gives a strong pretrained construction model with established reconstruction results, while keeping the proposed inference loop external to the policy architecture, training objective, and learned parameters. We also evaluate the same wrapper with other pretrained neural policies to characterize plug-and-play behavior.

\subsection{Baselines and Metrics}

We compare against simple construction heuristics, construction-only neural models, reconstruction methods, projection-based test-time generalization, learned improvement methods, and classical solvers. The primary metric is average optimality gap, complemented by solved count and runtime. Following the benchmark protocol in \citet{nrs_survey}, we include standard autoregressive and reconstruction baselines on the same instance groups. We additionally run TTPL locally on the shared TSPLIB and CVRPLIB instances using the same hardware environment as our method.

\subsection{Empirical Evidence for Query Alignment}

We hypothesize that bounded local transitions reduce OOD length extrapolation by keeping the neural horizon fixed and by attaching feedback to a smaller intervention unit. The evidence is organized around three diagnostics: inference scaling, memory-target sensitivity, and constrained-routing performance.

\textbf{Inference scaling.} Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} compares independent sampling, broad reconstruction, and the local transition loop under increasing inference budgets. Independent sampling saturates early, broad reconstruction improves but spends more effort per large query unit, and local transition inference continues to turn repeated calls into stateful guidance.

\textbf{Credit assignment.} Table~\ref{tab:memory-design} compares no memory, source-route memory, negative memory, and introduced-edge credit under the same local proposal and projection setting. Source-route memory reinforces edges present in a good reference trajectory, including many unchanged edges unrelated to the most recent improvement. Negative memory penalizes rejected proposals, although rejection can result from the selected state, neural sequence, projection width, or reference quality. \EAM{} uses the narrower credit target induced by local transition inference: new edges introduced by proposals that improve over the reference.

\textbf{Constraint-induced shift.} \CVRP{} introduces capacity and route-boundary interactions that make local feedback more informative than a broad scalar reconstruction outcome. Tables~\ref{tab:star-results} and~\ref{tab:cvrp-comparison} show that the method is competitive on \TSP{} at much lower runtime and improves most clearly over autoregressive construction and reconstruction baselines on \CVRP{}. The projection-baseline intersection in Table~\ref{tab:ttpl-overlap} is consistent with this interpretation: coordinate projection is highly effective for normalizing local input distributions, while local transition guidance is most beneficial when the model output must be validated against route-level constraints.

\subsection{Inference Scaling}

We hypothesize that bounding the neural horizon mitigates OOD length extrapolation and allows additional test-time compute to remain useful over longer inference budgets. Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} supports this hypothesis. Standard sampling from the pretrained policy improves only early and then saturates, because independent rollouts repeatedly reflect the same learned distribution. Broad reconstruction improves further, but the larger query unit makes each proposal expensive and weakens local credit assignment. In contrast, our approach produces a smoother improvement curve because each additional iteration creates local feedback that changes later proposals through \EAM{}.

Large $K$ improves local correction quality but increases local evaluation cost. \section{Discussion}

The results show that pretrained neural routing improves when the query distribution matches the scale at which the policy is reliable. The local mask changes the extrapolation problem: instead of decoding a route whose length grows with $n$, the model repeatedly evaluates bounded conditional transition states. \BTP{} converts plausible neural sequences into locally corrected candidates, preventing small geometric or feasibility defects from dominating proposal quality. \EAM{} transforms positive local improvements into a bounded prior over future actions, allowing repeated inference calls to accumulate instance-specific evidence.

The component ablation clarifies the division of labor. Without trajectory projection, neural proposals are too noisy to compete reliably with a strong reference trajectory. Without neural logits, \BTP{} can still improve routes, but it starts from weaker local rearrangements and does not reach the same gap. Without memory, the inference loop still benefits from local neural transitions, but it repeats similar proposals more often and loses the explicit mechanism for reusing positive local evidence. Performance depends on the interaction between learned proposal structure, local validation, and feedback-guided reuse.

The memory diagnostics show that introduced-edge credit is preferable to source-route reinforcement. Source-route memory reinforces edges present in a good route, including many unchanged edges that did not participate in the recent improvement. \EAM{} assigns credit to new edges introduced by an improving transition, matching the credit-assignment unit observed by the inference loop. The repeat-proposal and saturation diagnostics provide two practical checks: memory should reduce repeated low-value proposals without saturating quickly enough to overwrite the pretrained policy.

The scope sensitivity analysis identifies the main limit of the method. Very small transition budgets expose only minor local changes, while very large budgets approach broad reconstruction and increase both decoding noise and projection cost. Similarly, increasing $K$ improves \BTP{} only until the affected neighborhood is sufficiently covered. These trends support an intermediate regime in which the query remains local while preserving enough structure for the neural model to contribute.

The framework is a learned inference interface rather than a replacement for classical optimization. LKH-3 remains a strong optimization reference. The contribution is to show that a pretrained autoregressive model can obtain substantially better zero-shot behavior when test-time compute is organized around local transition inference rather than independent sampling or broad reconstruction. This positions inference-interface design as a complementary axis to architecture and training design in neural combinatorial optimization.

\section{Conclusion and Limitations}

We presented \method{}, a training-free inference guidance framework for pretrained autoregressive routing models. It uses a pretrained policy to propose bounded local transitions, applies \BTP{} as a local trajectory projection, and uses \EAM{} to store instance-specific credit for introduced edges with positive local advantage. Experiments on \TSP{} and \CVRP{} instances below 10K nodes show that this inference interface obtains competitive \TSP{} results at much lower runtime than high-budget autoregressive reconstruction, and stronger \CVRP{} results than the autoregressive baselines evaluated under the same pipeline. This constrained-routing gain is the clearest evidence for the proposed mechanism: when feasibility and cost depend on route-level interactions, local proposal validation and credit assignment become especially valuable.

The evaluation is limited to below-10K \TSP{} and \CVRP{} runs with \SIL{} as the base policy, with large-scale comparison reported through survey baselines and shared TTPL benchmark instances. Scaling the method to larger instances requires a hardware-controlled runtime protocol and a more detailed accounting of candidate-list construction, neural evaluation, and trajectory projection. Additional pretrained policies, multi-seed variance for stochastic proposal sampling, normalized runtime on shared hardware, and adaptive scope selection are important extensions of the same inference-interface thesis.

\subsection{Trajectory Projection Details}
\label{app:btp-details}

\BTP{} uses a checklist repair procedure adapted to the autoregressive transition setting. The checklist is initialized from the endpoints of edges deleted or inserted by the neural transition. Whenever a projected move is accepted, the endpoints whose incident edges changed are reinserted into the checklist. This mirrors the locality principle of SRR in \DyNACO{} while changing the proposal source from ACO perturbations to masked autoregressive transitions.

For \TSP{}, the only projection operator is candidate-graph 2-opt. For a checklist node $u$, \BTP{} scans at most $K$ nearest candidates $v\in\cN_K(u)$ and evaluates segment reversals that replace an incident edge of $u$, either $(u,\operatorname{succ}(u))$ or $(\operatorname{pred}(u),u)$, with a shorter connection to $v$ and the corresponding opposite endpoint. The best positive-gain reversal is applied, and the four affected endpoints are added back to the checklist. Thus the \TSP{} projection reaches a candidate-graph 2-opt fixed point inside the propagated affected region.

For \CVRP{}, \BTP{} first applies the same candidate-graph 2-opt logic within each route. It then scans cross-route candidate pairs touching the checklist and evaluates three capacity-preserving operators: relocating one customer into a neighboring route, swapping two customers across routes, and 2-opt* tail exchange between two routes. A final intra-route 2-opt pass is applied after cross-route changes because inter-route moves can create new within-route inversions. All \CVRP{} moves reject candidates that violate route capacity.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{\BTP{} for autoregressive transitions.}
\label{alg:btp}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Candidate trajectory $\hat y$, deleted edges $D$, inserted edges $I$, candidate lists $\cN_K$, move cap $h$
\STATE Initialize affected endpoints $A^{\mathrm{end}}$ from $D\cup I$
\STATE Build $\Omega=A^{\mathrm{end}}\cup\bigcup_{i\in A^{\mathrm{end}}}\cN_K(i)$
\STATE Initialize a checklist with nodes in $\Omega$
\WHILE{the checklist is nonempty and fewer than $h$ corrections have been accepted}
    \STATE Pop a checklist node $u$
    \IF{the instance is \TSP{}}
        \STATE Evaluate candidate-graph 2-opt reversals between the incident edges of $u$ and $\cN_K(u)$
    \ELSE
        \STATE Evaluate capacity-feasible intra-route 2-opt, inter-route relocate, swap, and 2-opt* moves touching $u$
    \ENDIF
    \IF{an improving scoped correction exists}
        \STATE Apply the best correction and add newly affected endpoints to the checklist
    \ELSE
        \STATE Mark $u$ as exhausted for the current pass
    \ENDIF
\ENDWHILE
\STATE \textbf{return} $\hat y$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Diagnostics and Compatibility}

\paragraph{Masked local decoding.}
The method depends on querying the declared pretrained policy under a local mask. When the model cannot yield logits for a masked state, the corresponding proposal is unsupported and should not be replaced by a non-neural fallback. This convention preserves a clear evaluation of the neural proposal mechanism and avoids conflating learned local transitions with handcrafted proposal rules.

\paragraph{Transition reach.}
When the transition budget is too small, repeated local proposals may tie the reference trajectory without escaping the current basin. Increasing the transition budget expands the reachable transition set, but also broadens the policy query and increases decoding difficulty. The sensitivity study therefore varies both $r$ and $K$.

\paragraph{Memory saturation.}
The clipping rule prevents unbounded memory growth, but saturation can still reduce adaptivity if many edges reach the clipping threshold early. The memory distribution in Figure~\ref{fig:memory-dist} is included as a diagnostic. If most nonzero entries saturate, the memory strength $\lambda$, step size $\eta$, or clipping threshold $M_{\max}$ should be reduced.

\paragraph{Projection locality.}
\BTP{} assumes that most damage from a bounded neural transition is near the deleted or inserted edges. If this assumption fails, the projection region may miss improvements that a global correction pass would find. The candidate width $K$ controls this tradeoff: larger values expand the projection neighborhood but increase the number of local evaluations.

\paragraph{Compatibility requirements.}
Applying the framework to a pretrained policy requires four implementation properties:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Masked local logits:} the model must expose a proposal distribution over a bounded feasible set.
  \item \textbf{Stable route context:} the model state must contain enough information to interpret route anchors and candidate nodes.
  \item \textbf{Feasible route transition:} the decoded local sequence must be applicable to the reference trajectory.
  \item \textbf{Problem-aware projection:} the projection step must preserve the constraints of the routing problem.
\end{itemize}
These requirements are mild for autoregressive \TSP{} construction models with explicit masks, but they become more restrictive for constrained routing variants or architectures that do not expose local logits.

\section{Design Rationale}

Constructive neural solvers learn a mapping from partial routing states to next-node decisions. Their main advantage is fast inference once a model has been trained. However, ordinary construction is irreversible: an early poor edge changes the future feasible set and forces later decisions to compensate. Beam search and sampling mitigate this issue by maintaining multiple trajectories, but they still use the model as a trajectory generator. The proposed framework retains the constructive decoder while changing its role. The model repeatedly evaluates small route-conditioned transition states, while the reference trajectory supplies context and the outer inference loop supplies feedback.

This design is related to reconstruction-style inference, which modifies part of a solution and queries a policy to complete the missing structure. Both approaches improve a reference trajectory through local intervention, but STAR uses a bounded route transition rather than a broad reconstruction. Large transitions increase decoding noise and projection cost, while very small transitions approach deterministic local correction. The method therefore operates in an intermediate regime in which the transition is local enough for immediate projection but structured enough for the neural model to contribute learned conditional information.

The memory representation also reflects this training-free guidance view. Actions are state-dependent: the same node choice can represent different structural changes depending on the current anchor and mask. Edges provide a more stable representation of route structure. \EAM{} therefore stores edge credit and maps it back to action scores through $\phi(s,a)$. This creates a lightweight memory that is independent of the internal parameterization of the pretrained policy.

Positive credit and monotone best-so-far tracking provide a conservative anytime output. Negative credit is tempting, but the signal from a failed proposal is ambiguous. A proposal may fail because the local context was poorly chosen, the neural sequence was weak, projection was too narrow, or the reference trajectory was already locally strong. Positive improvements provide clearer evidence: they identify transitions that survived projection and reduced cost. The reference trajectory can still move to the best projected proposal for exploration, while the returned best-so-far solution remains monotone.

\section{Extension to Capacitated Routing}

For \CVRP{}, we use a capacity-aware local state and projection operator. The local state includes depot position, route boundaries, customer demands, and remaining route capacity. The decoder mask prevents infeasible insertions. The projection step uses capacity-preserving local corrections. These requirements preserve the conceptual framework while adapting the feasibility machinery to capacitated routing.
